# History — The Brothers' Work and the Disgrace

## The brothers as researchers
Elias and Ezra Wren are not founders, not entrepreneurs, not commercial figures. **They are academics.** Their adult lives were spent in universities, laboratories, journals, conferences, peer review. The device was a research project, not a product. They were trying to publish, to be taken seriously, to be cited — not to sell.

- **Elias** trained in psychiatry. The mind, the disorder, the clinical treatment of psychological injury.
- **Ezra** trained in the engineering of mechanical and biological systems. The apparatus, the interface, the boundary where machine meets body.

Their collaboration was not just personal — it was disciplinary. Each of them brought something the other lacked. The device required both halves of a science the field treats as separate.

## The heretical theory
The brothers' core claim was simple and heretical:

> **Psychological injury can be treated by direct artificial intervention** — not only by talk, time, or pharmacology, but by entering the patient's mental state through a technical apparatus and operating on it from inside.

Beneath that claim sat the device they had built and the early results they had on themselves and a small number of patients. The theory was not vague speculation; it had hardware behind it.

The field met it with **scepticism.** The reasons were partly disciplinary (psychiatry does not need engineers; psychology has its own methods), partly philosophical (minds are not mechanical apparatus you intervene in directly), and partly methodological (extraordinary claims, extraordinary evidence). The brothers had results. The field would not look at them long enough to be convinced.

## The early device — what it lacked
The device worked, but was **initially crude.** The brothers did not yet know what safeguards a session needed; they would only learn what was needed after they found out the hard way. The current rig — the three chairs, the monitor's role, the exit phrase, the safety protocols — is a product of catastrophe, not foresight.

## The accident
Frustrated at not being believed, the brothers took **risks to prove themselves.** Sessions on each other beyond what they had reason to think was safe. Pushing into harder territory because if they could only get clean, repeatable results, the field would have to listen.

In one of those sessions, Ezra was the patient and Elias was the operator. It went wrong. **Ezra was injured in a way that required real medical treatment** — hospitalisation, neurology, the formal medical record. There was no way to keep what had happened off the books.

What the medical record showed was the visible damage. What it did not show — what the brothers could not say without ending themselves and the work — was *how* it had happened.

## The disgrace
The aftermath was bureaucratic and severe:

- **Elias's medical license was revoked** for human experimentation without proper ethics approval. The case was technically airtight: Ezra's injury was real, the lack of formal protocols was real, the ethical breach was real.
- **The brothers' research was discredited.** The same body that had previously dismissed their claims as fringe now had a moral reason to do so as well — and the discrediting was the more thorough for it.
- **No criminal charges.** The harm was within the family, the consent was technically there (Ezra was a willing participant), and the establishment had no appetite for prosecuting a theory it didn't believe in to begin with.

The establishment thinks it understands what happened: a brilliant-but-reckless psychiatrist and his engineer brother were doing crank-science on each other in defiance of ethics rules; one of them was harmed; the work was junk; case closed.

**They never believed the device actually worked.** That is why Elias still has it. Confiscation would have implied the device was a real medical instrument; classifying it as junk-science crank work meant nobody bothered.

## How it stayed quiet
**The story never became widely known.** The grief was so total to both brothers that neither fought it publicly. There was no court case to provide reporters with copy. The field had every reason to bury its own embarrassment quietly. The medical board did its work, the journals retracted, and everyone involved went home.

People who were close to the matter at the time still know. Older colleagues, the ethics board members from those years, a handful of journal editors. To them, "the Wren brothers" is the answer to a quiet professional question — *whatever happened to them?* — and the answer is: nothing good. They have not heard from Elias in decades. None of them know there is still a device, or that he is still doing the work, or that Ezra is alive in his own house.

## After the accident — the rebuilding (working timeline ≈ 20 years)
The years between the accident and the present have a clear shape, in four phases:

### Phase 1 — Care and reckoning (immediately after the accident)
- Ezra is hospitalised, then brought home. He cannot live independently and never will.
- **David and Alison Carter are hired** to care for him and to run the estate. Alison, freshly trained as a nurse, takes on Ezra's full-time care; David takes on the grounds and the farm. They were both in their early twenties. They have been on the property ever since. (See [nurse.md](nurse.md), [estate.md](estate.md).)
- Elias retreats from the world. He has lost his license, his standing, his brother. He does not see patients. He works.
- **He spends this period understanding why the device injured Ezra** — what part of the apparatus, what part of the protocol, what part of the operator's behaviour, was the actual cause. He realises the device needs **safeguards he had not known were needed**: the three-chair architecture, the monitor's role, the exit phrase, the safety protocols. He builds them in.

### Phase 2 — Animal testing (years of methodical work)
With safeguards in place, Elias begins **testing the device on animals.** Different species, in progression. The work is slow, rigorous, the kind of careful science he and Ezra had not done before the accident.

The animal phase culminates in **a chimpanzee.** Once the device demonstrates safety on a primate, Elias considers himself ready for human trials again. The animal testing is, in part, the work of a man trying to *earn* his right to use the device on people. It is also the work of a man preparing — eventually — to attempt his brother's recovery.

(Where the testing was done, what happened to the animals, whether records survive — TBD. The estate has outbuildings and grounds; David would have known animals were being kept, even if not why.)

### Phase 3 — The private sanitorium (small, exclusive, mild cases)
When Elias is ready for human trials, he opens a **private sanitorium** in the family home. The practice is:
- **Discreet, exclusive, and expensive.** Wealthy clients only, paid handsomely, kept quiet.
- **Mild cases.** Depression, anxiety, nervousness, nervous tics, stammering, phobias, conditions caused by environment or unresolved trauma. Never the catastrophic cases. Elias is rebuilding evidence, methodology, and confidence — he is not ready, and may never be ready, for the very damaged.
- **The hypnotherapy framing is established here.** This is when the deception of patients begins as a structured practice. The legal cover (hypnotherapy does not require a medical license) and the operational deception (the device is used while the patient is sedated, believing they were merely hypnotised) date from this phase.
- All of it is genuinely therapeutic, and all of it is also data. Each case teaches Elias more about how the device behaves, what it can reach, what it cannot, and — beneath all of it — what he might one day try with Ezra.

**Edith Halloran was an early patient of this phase.** Severely depressed after losing her husband and child, she was treated successfully. Grateful and with nowhere else she wanted to be, she **asked to stay and help.** Elias gave her the housekeeping and reception role. She has run the front of the house since.

### Phase 4 — The current residential clinic
Over time the practice expanded. The current operation — **up to six in-patients in residence at a time, weekly cycles, more substantial cases though still not the very worst** — is a more ambitious version of the sanitorium model. The caseload eventually grew past what Elias and Alison could handle as a duo at the device, and **Ashley was brought in as apprentice**, ostensibly to take pressure off the rig. (See [arc.md](arc.md) for game-start.)

Through every phase, the work has had two layers: the patients are genuinely helped, and the work is also a long path toward attempting Ezra's recovery. Both halves are true. The further along the path, the more the second half presses.

## The current legal position
Elias retains:
- Ownership of the family home and estate.
- His knowledge and skill.
- The device.

He does not retain:
- Medical license to practice psychiatry.
- Standing to publish, present, or be cited as a researcher.
- Any institutional protection.

The clinic operates as a "hypnotherapy practice." Hypnotherapy does not strictly require a medical license in most jurisdictions, so the framing offers a thin layer of plausibility. In reality, what is being done in his house is a vastly more serious matter — both ethically and legally. **Disclosure of the device's existence and use would mean prison**, not just a second disgrace. This is the practical reason the deception of patients is non-negotiable; it is also the reason Wren has trusted only Edith and Alison with the truth, and is wary of trusting Ashley further than they need to know.
