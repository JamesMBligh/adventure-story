# Scene — The Offer

*Narrated by Ashley.*

The Whitfield case had been on his desk all afternoon, the file open at the same page each time I passed his door. By the second pass, I knew what it was.

He always closed his files when he was working through a case alone. Today he had not closed it. He had even set a second chair across from his own.

When Mrs. Halloran came to clear the tea, Elias asked her to wait until after, and then he asked me to come and see him at eight.

I spent the hour between cleaning my hands of the day's monitor logs and sitting at the small table in the conservatory window of the library, watching the rain not quite commit to falling. Catherine Whitfield was on the third floor, two doors down from the gallery room. Five sessions in. Elias had operated three. I had observed two from the third chair, present and not present, watching him fold himself into the architecture of a woman who had not played a single note in fifteen months. The dream was a concert hall the size of a railway station and an audience that gathered without arriving. They had come close twice. The piano keys would not let her down.

She would be ready for the sixth session within the week. I had not asked who would operate it.

At eight, Elias was in the desk chair, the lamp low, the file open to the same page. He gestured at the second chair without looking up.

"Sit a moment."

I sat. The chair was leather, much used; it had been Ezra's once, Elias had said long ago in a lighter mood. It surprised me sometimes how often Elias's conversation had a gap exactly the shape of someone he never quite mentioned.

Elias closed the file. He took his glasses off and rubbed the bridge of his nose with two fingers. He looked at me for a long moment in the soft way he looked at people he was about to ask something of.

"How did you find Mrs. Whitfield this afternoon?"

"Settled. A little brighter. She sat through the second hour without being prompted."

"And in the gallery?"

"She walked the length of it twice. Stopped in front of the small Bonnard. I think she was looking at the hands."

He nodded, slowly — the way he did when something I had reported confirmed a thing he had already concluded for himself. Then he turned the lamp another notch lower, and the study composed itself around the warmer pool of light.

"I think she's near," he said. "I think she's close enough that the next session could be the one that releases her, if it is well done. Ashley." He met my eyes. "I think you should run the next session as the operator. I will monitor."

I had thought I had prepared for this. I had run it through in my head a hundred times in the last weeks — in the bathroom mirror, walking the lane by the south field, lying awake. I had thought I had prepared. The bottom of my stomach went out anyway.

"Yes," I said.

"You don't have to answer now."

"Yes," I said again, more carefully. "I — yes. I can. I'd like to."

Elias smiled. It was a smile I had seen him give Mrs. Halloran a few times, when she did something he found particular: a private softness through the long horizon of his face. "I want to ask you to think about it for a few minutes before we go further."

"Sir."

"You know why I'm asking you, and not asking — say — a colleague brought in for the purpose."

"Because I know the case."

"Yes. And."

I considered. "Because I have been inside the dream twice. And because you trust me."

"Yes." A pause. "There is something else. Something less to your credit. Will you let me say it?"

"Of course."

"You came to me," he said, "with eyes open. You read the things that have been written about me before you wrote to me. You read what the medical board concluded. You read the retracted papers. You came anyway. I have always understood that as a particular act of yours. Most people who come to this house come because they have been sent, or because grief has stopped letting them think. You came because grief had not stopped letting you think. You arrived here as a clinician, however young, and you chose this work. And so I trust you in it as a clinician. Not as a grateful patient." He lifted a hand a fraction off the desk, almost an apology. "Forgive me for being plain about that. But I want you to know that I am not — what is the word — I am not bringing you up because you owe me. I am bringing you up because I think your judgement is sound."

It took me a long second to find a voice. "Thank you," I said. I was not certain what I was thanking him for: the trust, or the honesty about it, or the fact that he had separated the two for me when I had not been able to do it myself.

"As to the case," he said, briskly, the way he transitioned out of anything close to feeling. "Tell me what you will be looking for."

"The teacher," I said. "The figure she described in the second interview. At first we'd assumed the dream was the stage and the audience and the keys, but in the third session, you walked her into the wings, and it was clear to me from the third chair that the wings are deeper than the stage. The teacher is somewhere in the wings. He's the thing she has been backing away from her whole career, and the keys won't move because the keys aren't actually it. The keys are what she lets herself be afraid of so that she does not have to be afraid of him."

"Yes."

"So I would not approach the keyboard at all."

"Where would you go?"

"Backstage. Behind the curtain that won't quite open. I think there is a corridor in there. I don't think she has ever consciously remembered what he told her, but I think the dream remembers, and the dream has put it down a corridor she has not yet had the company to walk."

"Company." He repeated the word, gently. "That's the right one."

"Yes."

"Good. Don't perform on the work, Ashley. Walk the corridor with her. Let her show you. The dream will not give you what she has not chosen to give you, but if you are present and will not flinch, what she has been carrying alone — not the teacher; what she said in the room she could not leave — will be lighter when she comes out. That is what we are doing. Not exorcism. Witness."

"Yes."

"And when you are finished. The phrase."

"*The light in the conservatory.*"

"Yes." His mouth softened. "Speak it. Don't let it be a thought. Speak it."

"I will."

He sat back. The lamp's pool of light made him look, for a moment, like a much older man. Outside, the rain had finally given up not falling and was now falling in earnest. Somewhere in the back of the house a door closed quietly: Mrs. Halloran going up to bed, or perhaps Alison crossing the corridor to the suite. I registered both possibilities without choosing between them. The house at this hour belonged to the people who knew it.

"There's one other thing," Elias said. He looked at the bookcase to his right, where between two volumes there was a small framed thing I had never been close enough to see. "It is — it doesn't matter. I was going to say something. But I think it can wait until you have done the work."

"Sir, if it would help me—"

"It wouldn't. Not on this case. I will tell you afterwards."

I hesitated. "Of me, or of the case?"

"Of me," Elias said.

There was a small silence. I did not press. I was learning, slowly, that there were sentences in this house that had to be allowed to start in their own time, and that pushing on them prematurely was the surest way to keep them locked in the mouth of the man trying to begin them.

"Tomorrow afternoon," Elias said. "I'll prepare her in the morning. You have the full file. Read it again. Don't read it tonight."

"All right."

"And Ashley."

"Sir."

He took his glasses up and put them back on. The look he gave me over them was the look of a clinician evaluating a junior, but with something beneath it that was not a clinician's at all. "You will be safe," he said. "You will always be safe. Do you remember the rule?"

"Yes."

"Tell me."

"The operator cannot be harmed. The patient can. The weight is mine."

"The weight is yours."

He nodded, once. We sat for a moment longer in the lamp's small pool — two clinicians at the end of a working day. The clock on the bookcase clicked over to half past. From the back of the house came a very faint sound that might have been someone speaking — a single syllable, indistinct. Elias did not turn his head, but his eyes flickered once, and I pretended for both of us not to have heard.

Then he said, "Goodnight," and I said, "Goodnight," and stood up and went.

In the hall, I stopped at the head of the stairs. Behind me, the study lamp went out. A floorboard, very softly, somewhere above. Whatever was awake in the house at this hour was awake elsewhere, as it always was. I put a hand on the banister and went up to my room, where I would not sleep until very late; where in the morning I would read the file again, as Elias had not asked; and where, in the afternoon, I would, for the first time, sit in the operator's chair.
