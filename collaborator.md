# The Collaborator — Ezra Wren

## Core
- **Name:** Ezra Wren.
- **Relation:** Elias Wren's **fraternal twin brother.** Not identical — two distinct men, born together, lifelong companions and complements.
- **Pet names between them:** **"Ez" and "El."** Childhood names, kept all their lives. The brothers are the only two people in the world who use these names. Elias is now the only one who *can*. He almost never says "Ez" aloud, but when he does — at the door of a closed room, late at night, in an unguarded moment with Ashley — those two syllables are the loudest thing in the house. And no one has called Elias "El" in years.

## Status (load-bearing facts)
- **Where Elias was drawn to the mind, Ezra was drawn to how things work** — the engineering of mechanical and biological systems. He became a builder of physical apparatus and a knower of the body that meets it.
- **The device is the literal expression of both of their skills combined.** Elias contributed the psychiatry and the theory of dream-walking; Ezra contributed the engineering — the rig, the headset, the sedation protocols, the biological interface, the entire machine. **Neither of them could have built it alone.**
- The brothers were **academic researchers** — universities, journals, peer review — pushing a heretical theory the field would not believe (see `history.md`).
- During development, the brothers **took turns being the patient and the operator**, testing the device on each other. The early device was crude and lacked safeguards they did not yet know were needed.
- **Frustration at not being believed pushed them to take risks.** They ran sessions beyond what they had reason to think was safe, hoping to produce results clean enough to force the field to look.
- During one of those risk-taking sessions, **Ezra was the patient.** Elias was the operator. The session went wrong. Ezra was injured.
- The injury required real medical treatment. He was hospitalised. The medical record was created and could not be hidden. **The accident is what cost Elias his license** — human experimentation without ethics approval, evidenced by the harm to his own brother — and what discredited the brothers' research.
- Ezra is **alive but in a severely impacted mental state.** Whatever Elias did inside that dream, or could not do, or could not reach, broke him. He has not recovered.

## What the brothers were doing that day
A risk-taking session, beyond the safety protocols they would later understand were necessary. Specifics TBD — was it a planned push at a known limit, an exploratory experiment in unfamiliar territory, or simply more of the same with the line happening to break that time? But the *frame* is fixed: they were pushing because the field would not listen, and they paid the price the field had been telling them they would.

## He is the engine of everything Wren does
The clinic, the device's continued operation, the secrecy, the patient deception, the relentless caseload, the willingness to break safety rules — **all of it is in service of finding a way to undo what he did to Ezra.** Wren is not running a practice that incidentally serves his obsession. He is running a project to recover Ezra, that incidentally heals patients along the way. Every successful case is a step on the path. Every patient is a station of the cross.

This is also why Wren takes risks no responsible practitioner would: he has already lost the person who has been at his side since they shared a womb. The calculus of caution is broken; he has nothing left to be careful about that compares.

## A particular kind of grief
Losing a twin, even a fraternal one, is its own kind of loss. The two brothers had been a unit since before either was a self. To break Ezra's mind is, for Elias, to have broken half of his own life — the half that knew him before he knew himself. He is incomplete without him in a structural sense, not a sentimental one.

## A practical consequence
**Elias does not understand the device's engineering as well as Ezra did.** He has been keeping it running for years from Ezra's notebooks, drawings, and what he himself absorbed by osmosis. Some of what makes the rig work, only Ezra truly knew. The device works, but the deepest knowledge of it died inside the mind it broke. This is why Wren cannot simply build a *new* device; the original is irreplaceable. It is also a slow ticking clock — every component that fails is one Wren may not be able to repair without Ezra.

## Why this is the spine of the story
- The asymmetry of risk: **the operator is structurally safe; the patient is not.** Wren is the operator whose hands left someone he loved devastated. Every session he runs since has been with the same hands.
- Wren is the proof that the operator walks out, and the perpetrator of the worst thing the device has done. Both at once.
- His silence about the collaborator is **guilt as much as grief**, with a specific shape: he was inside their dream when it happened.
- And the silence is also **strategy**. He cannot speak of the collaborator because doing so would expose the whole project — that everything he has built since is a long-running effort to undo what he did, with patients he is using, however gently, as means.

## What it teaches the story (the rule it sets)
- **Operators cannot be harmed by the device.** Ashley is, in every session, physically and mentally safe. They will always come out.
- **Patients can be harmed.** What the operator does inside the dream — including doing nothing, missing something, being too slow, going too far — translates to real damage to the patient's waking mind.
- This makes the ethical position acute: Ashley operates from a position of guaranteed protection over a person in total vulnerability. The patient is sedated, deceived, dreaming. Ashley is awake, choosing.

## Where he is now — the parents' suite
**Ezra lives in what was once the brothers' parents' suite**, in a separate area of the house closed to patients and to Ashley. A self-contained set of rooms: a large bedroom, a parlour, a study, a separate bathroom, and a large enclosed conservatory along the back, full of plants and looking out over the estate. The house itself is the home the brothers grew up in. Elias did not buy a building and turn it into a clinic; he transformed the home where he and Ezra were raised into the apparatus of his guilt, and put Ezra to live in their parents' rooms.

He has been in this state for **roughly twenty years**, since the accident.

The conservatory matters. It is full of light and living things; he can move through it safely; the plants give him a slow, organic sensory environment that does not amplify what is already happening in his mind.

- **Mrs. Halloran knows about Ezra and has known since her own treatment, decades ago** (see `clinic.md`). She was healed by the brothers when the device was still experimental; she knew Ezra when he was well.
- **The Nurse** (see `nurse.md`) attends to Ezra daily. He is her primary charge; the in-patients are a secondary duty.
- **Ashley does not know Ezra exists.** They are aware that there is a part of the house they do not go into. They are curious. They do not press. This curiosity is one of the engines of the player's arc.

## The shape of his damage — load-bearing
**Ezra's mind is shattered.** Not silent, not absent, not locked in. Shattered.

He is **not a danger** — not to himself, not to others. He can perform the most basic self-care: dress himself, eat the food set in front of him, walk through the suite, sit in the conservatory. He is gentle. He is, in some sense, still recognisably Ezra.

But his mind will not focus. It is **constantly shifting** between his present, his memories, and his imagination, with no partition between them. He hallucinates and hears voices. He sees things that are not there and does not know they are not there. He remembers things as though they are happening now. He invents things and lives inside them while they last. To him, these states are indistinguishable. He cannot sort the room he is sitting in from the room he sat in as a child from the room he is dreaming. His consciousness has lost the rule that lets the rest of us tell which is which.

He can talk. He can be talked to. He sometimes recognises Elias and calls him **El.** He sometimes does not. He sometimes speaks in fluent sentences whose meaning is untrackable to anyone but him. He sometimes gives an answer to a question no one has asked. There are brief windows of partial lucidity in which he is, for a moment, almost present — and then he is gone again. Elias spends every one of those windows that he can.

## Why this kind of damage is the device's signature
Ezra's condition is **what the device produces in patients during a session — present, memory, and imagination overlaid without partition — running constantly in his own waking mind.** Whatever happened in the dream that day left him in the dream-state permanently, but without the device, without sedation, without the boundary the rig usually maintains. He is dreaming awake.

This is also why ordinary psychiatry cannot help him: there is no continuity to grab onto. Talk therapy requires a coherent surface. He has none. The only instrument that has any chance of matching the shape of the damage is the device itself — going into his mind and finding a way to help it organize itself again. This is what Elias has been trying to learn how to do, on other people, for years.

## What Ashley not knowing means
- Ashley believes the work is what it appears to be: helping patients, paid for by referral and word-of-mouth, run out of Wren's home. Ashley does not yet see the second layer.
- The closed area, the staff she does not interrogate, Wren's unguarded "Ez" overheard once or twice — all of these have settled in Ashley as a low background noise of *something I am not being told*. Most days they accept it as part of working with a private man.
- The story can pull, slowly or suddenly, on the moment Ashley realizes the work has had a private agenda all along — and that they are being trained to do something they have not consented to.

## How Ezra is recovered, eventually — narrative direction
Late in the story, **Ashley is the one who operates on Ezra.** The role assignments:

- **Ashley** — the operator. The only person in the house who *can* do it.
- **Alison** — willing to monitor, but **refuses to operate the device** at all. Has cared for the man it broke; will not sit in the chair that can be put in his. This is fixed.
- **Elias** — refuses to operate on Ezra. Because of what happened. He has not been the active hands inside Ezra's dream since the day he came back alone, and he will not be again.

So the recovery falls to Ashley. The apprentice is the one who will undo what the master could not. See `arc.md`.

The first Ezra session is **reassembling the shards** — gathering the fragments of his consciousness, restoring some partition between present, memory, and imagination. Each subsequent Ezra session is a chapter boundary in the larger story; each one brings him a little further back and reveals a little more of the brothers' history. By the end, Ezra is healed.

## Open questions
- **What the brothers were doing** that fateful session. A routine test gone wrong, or a deliberate push at the device's edge?
- **The brothers' shared pre-history** — childhood, parents, the moment one chose mind and the other chose machine, when the collaboration began.
- **Has Elias attempted recovery on Ezra alone before, in the years since?** Or has he ruled himself out from the moment he came back? (His refusal in the late-game suggests he ruled himself out long ago, but it could also be that earlier failed attempts hardened the refusal.)
