# The Clinic

## Setting
- **Time:** Present day.
- **Place:** A large home with grounds, **owned by Elias Wren**, near (but not in) an unspecified city.
- **Type:** A converted house. Wren lives there. The clinic is part of his private home.

## Patient experience
- Patients attend as **in-patients for a week**.
- They are told they are undergoing **hypnotherapy** for their condition.
- During each session they are hypnotised, then **sedated**, then **connected to the device without their knowledge**.
- They wake later believing the hypnosis worked.
- **The patient never knows the device exists.** Their consent is for hypnosis, not for what is actually happening.

## This is the central moral fault line
This is the load-bearing ethical fact of the whole story. Wren's "disgrace at the hands of the establishment" gets reframed by this: the establishment may have been correct to be uneasy with him, or Wren may have decided non-disclosure was the only way to do work that actually helps people. **Both are true at once.** The story will have to walk this line, not resolve it cheaply.

Implications:
- Ashley is now complicit in the deception of every new patient.
- The deception is the practical reason secrecy exists — discovery means prison, not just disgrace.
- A patient learning the truth, mid-treatment or after, is a permanent narrative threat.
- Ashley's own treatment was performed under the same deception — they consented to "hypnosis" and got something else. They learned the truth later, and accepted Wren anyway. That acceptance is fragile under pressure.

## Atmosphere
**Bookish and analog.** Paper notes. Glass instruments. Low lamplight. A record player. Wren is a man who writes by hand. The technology of the device sits incongruously in a setting that otherwise feels Victorian-by-temperament — the future hidden inside the 19th century.

## Layout (working)
- **The house:** consulting rooms, library, Wren's study, dining room. Public-facing.
- **Patient quarters:** modest in-patient rooms (likely upstairs), enough for a small caseload at any time.
- **The treatment room:** where the device lives. Hidden, restricted, the heart of the operation.
- **The grounds:** a perimeter that gives privacy and pacing room — patients walk; Ashley and Wren walk between sessions.

## The house — Wren family home
This is not a purpose-built clinic. It is the house Elias and Ezra **grew up in**. Their parents lived and died here. Elias inherited it and converted the public part of it into a private practice without ever leaving. He did not buy a building to set up a clinic; he transformed the home where he and his twin were raised. Ezra now lives in their parents' suite. Patients sleep in rooms that may once have been the brothers' own.

This matters. Every corner of the house has a memory in it that is not the present. The building itself is the same kind of palimpsest as Ezra's mind.

The wider property is a **working commercial farm**, run by the Groundskeeper. See `estate.md` for the property overall.

## Layout — and the secret area
The house has two zones:

- **The open clinic.** Consulting rooms, library, dining room, patient quarters, the treatment room where the device lives. This is where Ashley moves. Up to six patients in residence at any time.
- **The parents' suite, closed to Ashley.** A self-contained area of the house — a large bedroom, parlour, study, separate bathroom, and a large enclosed conservatory full of plants and looking out over the estate. **Ezra lives here.** Wren and the staff (Edith, the Nurse) go in. Ashley does not. They have never been told what is there. They have noticed; they are **curious about it but do not press**, because pressing would breach the only thing about Wren that is unambiguously off-limits.

## Cast at the clinic
- **Elias Wren** — owner, lives in the main house.
- **Ashley** — apprentice and operator. Lives in the main house, has a room there. Knows about the device and the deception of patients. **Does not know about Ezra.** Curious about the closed area but does not ask. Trusted with the operating chair but not with the truth of why the work exists.
- **Mrs. Edith Halloran** — long-time housekeeper and receptionist, lives in the main house. Came to **Elias** as a patient years after the accident, severely depressed after losing her **husband and child**. She was an **early patient of the post-accident sanitorium phase** (see `history.md`), treated successfully, and asked to stay and help. She has run the front of the house since.
  - She **knows about the device** — she experienced it herself, was told the truth in the years that followed, and has been part of the inner circle ever since.
  - She **knows about Ezra.** She has known for years. She did not know him when he was well; he was already broken before she arrived. He is a fact of the house, not a memory.
  - She **does not tell Ashley.** She has watched Ashley arrive as a patient, be treated, become apprentice, be trained on the device — all without ever revealing what is in the closed area. This is something she carries. She and Wren have agreed on this silence. Whether she fully agrees with it, she has never said.
- **Alison Carter** — the Nurse (`nurse.md`), early forties, formally trained. Lives in the **converted stables** with her husband David and their son Ryan (fifteen, at boarding school most of the year). Long-standing employee with great affection and trust for both brothers, built up over years of close association. **No-nonsense, disciplined, briskly efficient with patients who indulge in self-pity; surprisingly soft when caring for Ezra.** To the six in-patients she presents as a house matron — light supervision, meals, medication, the daily check-ins. Her real job is **constant attendance on Ezra** in the closed parents' suite.
- **David Carter** — the Groundskeeper, Alison's husband, also early forties. Manages the estate and the commercial farm. Lives in the converted stables. Knows about Ezra but believes (with the rest of the world) that it was a stroke or breakdown decades ago. **Does not know about the device.**

## How the house actually runs
**Alison and Edith effectively run the house between them**, by long-established alliance and clear division. Alison handles the medical and clinical side: patient nursing, Ezra's daily care, anything with a body in it. Edith handles the domestic and hospitality side: bookings, meals, the front door, the rhythms of normalcy. Neither steps on the other's territory; both enforce the demarcation. They get along well in the manner of two competent professionals who know each other is good. Both are proud of what they do.

Where they differ deeply: **Edith lost her family and the house became her family**, and she never goes off duty. **Alison has her own family in the stables**, and shuts a door at the end of the day. That difference is the reason the operation hums; it is also the reason their futures with the house, if it ever broke open, would diverge.
- **Ryan Carter** — the Carters' fifteen-year-old son. At boarding school most of the year. When home for holidays, the household rhythm changes — he runs around the grounds, eats with his parents in the stables, and is reminded by his mother to give the closed wing of the main house a wide berth.

## The structure of who knows what
Concentric rings of awareness:

- **Wren** — knows everything, including the cost of every layer of the deception.
- **The inner circle (Mrs. Halloran, the Nurse)** — know about the device and about Ezra. Have held the silence with Wren for years.
- **Ashley** — knows about the device and the patient deception, but not about Ezra.
- **The Groundskeeper** — knows there is an unwell brother in the main house. Does not know about the device.
- **The Nurse and Groundskeeper's son** — knows there is a man he doesn't see and a wing he doesn't go into. Knows not to discuss family business outside the estate.
- **The patients in residence** — know nothing. Believe they are receiving hypnotherapy.
- **The world** — knows the property as a working farm with a discreet medical practice attached.

Ashley is the most-trusted *operator* and the least-trusted *intimate*. That asymmetry is one of the story's deepest discoveries.

## How patients arrive
**Mixed / opportunistic.** Wren takes referrals from a thin network of old colleagues who quietly send him cases they can't help. He also takes word-of-mouth patients passed along by former patients (within tight discretion). And he hunts. He reads case journals, news, court records, looking for cases that catch him — and reaches out. The mix is significant: the "natural" caseload is interspersed with patients Wren has chosen for his own reasons, which the player will sometimes be able to feel.

## Wren's justification for the deception
**Disclosure is impossible.** Wren's stated position: there is no version of this work that survives being documented. Even an informed-consent form is evidence of crimes that would put him and Ashley in prison and shut the work down forever. Secrecy is therefore not a preference but a precondition — the patients can't be told, because being able to tell them would mean the work could not exist.

The legal stakes are real. Elias has already lost his medical license over Ezra's injury (see `history.md`). A second incident of unauthorized human experimentation — especially one shown to involve a *device* the establishment thought was junk-science — would not end with another revocation. It would end with prison. The clinic operates under a hypnotherapy framing because hypnotherapy is one of the few practices that can be conducted without a medical license; everything else they do has no legal cover at all.

This is the rationalization Ashley has accepted. It's also self-serving. Wren never quite says it, but: documentation would mean prison.

## Caseload and rhythm
**Up to six patients in residence at any one time.** This is also (working assumption) the device's cap on simultaneous dream-worlds — it has matched a small number all along, and Wren has organized the clinic around it.

Six is enough that:
- Patients see each other at meals, in the garden, in the hallways.
- They are strangers to each other but know they share something.
- Ashley moves between cases during a week; the work has rhythm, overlap, and triage.
- Wren is responsible for the welfare of a small ensemble at all times.

## Open questions

- The exact rhythm of a patient's week: number of "hypnosis" sessions, talking time with Wren, free time, walks on the grounds.
- Does each patient get exactly one dream-session, or several over the week?
- Is there ever overlap of a patient's stay with the patient who recommended them?
